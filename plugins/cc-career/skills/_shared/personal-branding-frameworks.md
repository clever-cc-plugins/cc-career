# Personal Branding Frameworks — Shared Reference

Shared reference for `personal-branding-strategy`. Apply a framework whenever the
task involves articulating how a person wants to be perceived professionally, or the
themes their public output (posts, talks, writing) should reinforce. For a narrowly
scoped ask — e.g. rewriting a single bio line — skip this file and answer directly.

## How to select

1. **Identify the branding challenge** from the brief (e.g. clarify a scattered
   online presence, decide what to be known for before a job search, prepare talking
   points ahead of speaking engagements).
2. **Note the available context** — career background and goals, and career-plan.md
   if registered (prefer its Career Anchor finding as an input, not a re-derivation),
   plus a target audience if one is already evident from career goals.
3. **Check for project preferences** — a recorded rejected framing in
   `.claude/learnings.md` wins over this file's default.
4. **Find the matching framework(s)** using the descriptions below. Golden Circle and
   Personal SWOT are the default pair — Golden Circle sets the message, Personal SWOT
   stress-tests it against reality. Add Strategic Authenticity Calibration when the
   draft message risks reading either as generic corporate-speak or as
   oversharing — state each framework choice separately.
5. **State the choice** before generating: `"Using [framework] because [one-sentence
reason]."`

## Golden Circle (Simon Sinek), applied personally

**Ground the brand message in Why before How or What**

What it is: Sinek's model orders communication from the inside out — Why (the
belief or purpose driving the work), How (the distinctive approach), What (the
visible output: job title, projects, content). Applied to a person rather than a
company, it prevents a personal brand from collapsing into a job-title list.

Best for: The first branding pass for someone whose current online presence is just
a resume restated; clarifying the throughline across a varied career; giving
cc-content skills a "Why" to keep every piece of content on-message.

Avoid: A person who already has a clear, working Why and just needs tactical themes
— skip straight to naming the themes.

Note: Why-first ordering is a communication heuristic, not an empirically validated
model — treat it as a drafting tool, not a claim about what actually motivates
audiences. In technical or B2B contexts, an audience often weighs demonstrated
competence over stated purpose; naming the target audience first (below) keeps the
Why grounded in who has to find it credible, rather than staying abstract.

How to apply: (1) Name the target audience this brand is for — a specific role,
niche, or decision-maker (e.g. "engineering managers hiring for platform roles," not
"people online") — and the problem or question they have that this person can
credibly answer; if the loaded career goals don't already make this clear, ask one
direct question rather than assuming. (2) From the loaded career background,
career-plan (if present, its Career Anchor), and that audience, draft one Why
sentence — the belief, not the job. (3) Draft one How sentence — the distinctive
method or lens. (4) List the current and near-term What — roles, projects, content —
and check each still traces back to the Why; flag any that don't.

## Personal SWOT

**Stress-test the brand message against internal and external reality**

What it is: A four-quadrant audit — Strengths and Weaknesses (internal, about the
person), Opportunities and Threats (external, about the market or industry they
operate in). Applied after a Why/How/What draft, it catches an aspirational brand
message that isn't yet backed by evidence, or that ignores a real market shift.

Best for: Pressure-testing a draft brand message before publishing; identifying
which Strength to lead with when several are true; flagging a Threat (e.g. an
oversaturated niche) the person should position around rather than into.

Avoid: A first pass with no draft message yet to test — run Golden Circle first.

Note: this is a self-report exercise, so Strengths in particular skew toward what
the person already believes about themselves, not necessarily what others notice.
If the person has ready access to a handful of trusted colleagues' honest input (or
mentions performance-review feedback, testimonials, or recurring compliments), fold
that in as the Strengths source instead of pure self-assessment — this catches
blind spots self-report misses. Don't ask the person to go and collect it now; use
what's already at hand.

How to apply: (1) List 2-4 Strengths from the loaded career background that are
demonstrable, not aspirational — prefer ones corroborated by others' feedback where
that's available. (2) List 1-2 Weaknesses honestly — gaps the brand message should
not overclaim past. (3) List 1-2 Opportunities in the person's market, naming who
else occupies the space (a niche going underserved relative to 2-3 peer or
competitor archetypes, a format gaining reach). (4) List 1-2 Threats (a crowded
niche, a skill becoming commoditized). (5) Adjust the draft brand themes so they
lead with a Strength that meets an Opportunity, differentiate against the named
peer archetypes, and avoid overclaiming past a named Weakness.

## Strategic Authenticity Calibration

**Set how much personal disclosure the themes should carry**

What it is: Personal branding advice pulls toward two failure modes — themes so
polished they read as generic corporate messaging nobody trusts, or themes that lean
on vulnerability and personal story past what the person is comfortable sustaining
publicly. This framework names the tension explicitly and picks a deliberate point
on that spectrum, rather than defaulting to either extreme.

Best for: A draft Why/How/What or theme set that reads as flat and interchangeable
with anyone else's ("passionate about delivering results"); or the opposite, a
person eager to lead with personal narrative who hasn't considered what stays
private. Also useful once themes are set, to decide the personal-to-expertise ratio
future content should follow.

Avoid: Running this before a Why/How/What draft exists — there's nothing yet to
calibrate.

How to apply: (1) Name where each draft theme currently sits — closer to "safe and
generic" or closer to "exposed and personal." (2) Ask what the person is actually
willing to sustain publicly and consistently, not just comfortable saying once. (3)
Adjust themes toward more concrete specificity and honest opinion (not more
disclosure) to fix genericness; strip specifics that reveal more than the person
wants ongoing exposure on to fix oversharing. (4) Note the resulting balance — e.g.
"mostly hands-on expertise, occasional named lesson-learned, no personal-life
content" — so `cc-content` skills know the register to write in.
