# Career Planning Frameworks — Shared Reference

Shared reference for `career-plan`. Apply a framework whenever the task involves
structuring a multi-month or multi-year career direction, an uplevel roadmap, or a
skill-development plan. For a narrowly scoped ask — e.g. "just list three courses I
should take" — skip this file and answer directly.

## How to select

1. **Identify the planning challenge** from the brief (e.g. choose a direction among
   options, prioritize which skills to build first, structure a promotion timeline).
2. **Note the available context** — career background and goals from `career-onboarding`,
   and a personal-branding-strategy.md if registered.
3. **Check for project preferences** — if `.claude/learnings.md` records a preferred
   framework or a rejected one, that preference wins over this file's default.
4. **Find the matching framework** using the descriptions below.
5. **State the choice** before generating: `"Using [framework] because [one-sentence
reason]."` Career Anchors and the Skill-Will Matrix are almost always applied
   together — Career Anchors sets the direction, Skill-Will Matrix sequences the
   near-term work — with Job Crafting added only when the direction points outside
   the current role. State each choice separately when more than one applies.

## Career Anchors (Edgar Schein)

**Identify the stable, non-negotiable driver(s) behind career choices**

What it is: Schein's model holds that career choices are shaped by "anchors" —
combinations of self-perceived talent, values, and motives that a person will not
trade away, even for a promotion or a raise. The eight anchors: Technical/Functional
Competence, General Managerial Competence, Autonomy/Independence, Security/Stability,
Entrepreneurial Creativity, Service/Dedication to a Cause, Pure Challenge, and
Lifestyle. Most people carry two or three of these at once rather than a single
dominant one — treat "one clean anchor" as the exception, not the default. A plan
that points away from a real anchor tends to fail even when every milestone is met.

Best for: Setting the long-term direction of a career plan; resolving a fork between
two plausible paths (e.g. management track vs. deepening technical expertise);
explaining why a past role that looked good on paper felt wrong.

Avoid: Short-term or single-skill planning asks where direction isn't in question.

How to apply: (1) From the loaded career background and goals, identify 1-3 candidate
anchors the person's own account of satisfying vs. unsatisfying past work points to —
Schein designed this as a conversational, evidence-from-history diagnosis, not a
checklist self-score, so infer from what they've described rather than asking them to
pick from the list. (2) Name the candidate anchor(s) and one sentence of evidence for
each, and state them as an inference to confirm rather than a verdict. (3) Use the
strongest-evidenced anchor to resolve any direction fork in the plan, and flag if the
stated goal pulls against any of the candidates.

## Job Crafting (Wrzesniewski & Dutton)

**Check whether the current role can be reshaped before recommending a new one**

What it is: Job crafting is proactively redrawing the boundaries of an existing role
along three dimensions — task crafting (changing the scope or mix of
responsibilities), relational crafting (changing who you work with or how), and
cognitive crafting (reframing how the work's purpose is understood). It's the
lower-risk, faster-to-execute alternative that direction-setting plans tend to skip
past on the way to "get a new role" or "change employers."

Best for: The moment a plan's direction implies leaving the current role or employer.
Before locking that in, checking whether redesigning the current role could reach the
same goal without the switching cost.

Avoid: Directions that already stay within the current role, or goals that
structurally require a different employer (e.g. a regulated title, a pay band the
current org can't offer). Don't force a crafting detour when the goal doesn't need it.

How to apply: (1) Only trigger this when the plan's direction points outside the
current role or employer. (2) Ask, briefly, whether task, relational, or cognitive
crafting inside the current role could close part of the gap — e.g. taking on a
stretch responsibility, building a relationship with a target team, or reframing an
existing task toward the target skill. (3) If a viable crafting move exists, note it
as a near-term, lower-risk milestone alongside (not instead of) the larger direction;
if none applies, say so in one line and move on.

## Skill-Will Matrix

**Sequence near-term development by current skill level and motivation**

What it is: A 2x2 that plots each candidate skill or responsibility on two axes —
current skill level (low/high) and current will/motivation to build it (low/high).
High-skill/high-will items are ready to take on now. Low-skill/high-will items are
the best investment for near-term development (motivation carries the learning
curve). High-skill/low-will items are worth delegating or dropping from the plan.
Low-skill/low-will items should not be prioritized unless the goal requires them.

Best for: Turning a broad goal into a sequenced, near-term development plan;
deciding what to build first among several candidate skills; making the case for
why one skill is prioritized over another the person also mentioned.

Avoid: Long-range direction-setting (use Career Anchors for that first).

How to apply: (1) List 3-6 candidate skills or responsibilities implied by the
stated goal. (2) Plot each on skill level and will, inferred from the loaded career
background (explicit statements of "I enjoy X" or "I've done Y for years" are strong
signals). (3) Sequence the plan: low-skill/high-will first, high-skill/high-will
next, note high-skill/low-will as delegate-or-drop, omit low-skill/low-will unless
required.
