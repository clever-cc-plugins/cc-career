# Career Planning Frameworks — Shared Reference

Shared reference for `career-plan`. Apply a framework whenever the task involves
structuring a multi-month or multi-year career direction, an uplevel roadmap, or a
skill-development plan. For a narrowly scoped ask — e.g. "just list three courses I
should take" — skip this file and answer directly.

## How to select

1. **Identify the planning challenge** from the brief (e.g. choose a direction among
   options, prioritize which skills to build first, structure a promotion timeline).
2. **Note the available context** — career background and goals from `career-onboarding`,
   and a personal-branding-strategy.md if registered.
3. **Check for project preferences** — if `.claude/learnings.md` records a preferred
   framework or a rejected one, that preference wins over this file's default.
4. **Find the matching framework** using the descriptions below.
5. **State the choice** before generating: `"Using [framework] because [one-sentence
reason]."` Both frameworks are usually applied together — Career Anchors sets the
   direction, Skill-Will Matrix sequences the near-term work — state each choice
   separately when both apply.

---

## Career Anchors (Edgar Schein)

**Identify the stable, non-negotiable driver behind career choices**

What it is: Schein's model holds that everyone has one dominant "anchor" — a
combination of self-perceived talent, values, and motives that a person will not
trade away, even for a promotion or a raise. The eight anchors: Technical/Functional
Competence, General Managerial Competence, Autonomy/Independence, Security/Stability,
Entrepreneurial Creativity, Service/Dedication to a Cause, Pure Challenge, and
Lifestyle. A plan that points away from the person's anchor tends to fail even when
every milestone is met.

Best for: Setting the long-term direction of a career plan; resolving a fork between
two plausible paths (e.g. management track vs. deepening technical expertise);
explaining why a past role that looked good on paper felt wrong.

Avoid: Short-term or single-skill planning asks where direction isn't in question.

How to apply: (1) From the loaded career background and goals, identify the anchor
the person's own account of satisfying vs. unsatisfying past work points to — do not
ask the person to self-diagnose from the list; infer it and state the inference for
confirmation. (2) Name the anchor and one sentence of evidence for it. (3) Use it to
resolve any direction fork in the plan, and flag if the stated goal pulls against it.

## Skill-Will Matrix

**Sequence near-term development by current skill level and motivation**

What it is: A 2x2 that plots each candidate skill or responsibility on two axes —
current skill level (low/high) and current will/motivation to build it (low/high).
High-skill/high-will items are ready to take on now. Low-skill/high-will items are
the best investment for near-term development (motivation carries the learning
curve). High-skill/low-will items are worth delegating or dropping from the plan.
Low-skill/low-will items should not be prioritized unless the goal requires them.

Best for: Turning a broad goal into a sequenced, near-term development plan;
deciding what to build first among several candidate skills; making the case for
why one skill is prioritized over another the person also mentioned.

Avoid: Long-range direction-setting (use Career Anchors for that first).

How to apply: (1) List 3-6 candidate skills or responsibilities implied by the
stated goal. (2) Plot each on skill level and will, inferred from the loaded career
background (explicit statements of "I enjoy X" or "I've done Y for years" are strong
signals). (3) Sequence the plan: low-skill/high-will first, high-skill/high-will
next, note high-skill/low-will as delegate-or-drop, omit low-skill/low-will unless
required.
